Heat, Health and Young People in Karnataka

Building a youth-responsive, heat-resilient policy ecosystem. Research with young people across urban, coastal, tribal and drought-prone Karnataka.

Watch the filmYoung people on living with heatHeat, Health and Young People in Karnataka · Click to play
Executive summary

Heat stress is no longer an episodic hazard in Karnataka. It is a public health, development and governance challenge reshaping young people's lives.

Rising temperatures, changing weather, water scarcity and urban heat islands are changing how young people study, work, travel, earn a living and look after their physical and mental wellbeing. The effects go well beyond discomfort, reaching education, productivity, health and their hopes for the future.

This evidence paper, by Restless Development with Swasti, The Health Catalyst, asks how heat is experienced by young people across Karnataka, and what a youth-responsive policy ecosystem would look like.

The central finding

Heat is a risk multiplier. It does not act alone. It amplifies inequalities of poverty, work, housing, water and geography.

Young people in farming, fishing, construction, informal work and long commutes face heightened exposure, while those in poorly ventilated urban settlements carry compounded thermal stress from weak cooling infrastructure and shrinking green cover.

How we listened

Four settings, four realities

The study brought together an evidence synthesis, listening exercises with young people, expert insights and a youth co-design workshop, across four settings that reflect Karnataka's climatic and developmental diversity.

Urban

Bengaluru

Hotter summers, unequal access to cooling and long commutes. Some households can afford air conditioning; others rely on public shade. Young people stressed protecting trees, lakes and green spaces.

Coastal

Honnavar

Young fisherfolk feel heat through humidity, direct sun at sea and changing marine life. They shift fishing hours, combine forecasts with traditional knowledge, and share drinking water and support at sea.

Tribal

HD Kote

Heat is read through ecological change: shrinking forests, shifting rainfall, lost biodiversity and disappearing traditional foods. Indigenous knowledge still shapes healthcare and adaptation.

Arid agrarian

Yadgir and Kalaburagi

Heat is an unavoidable reality. Young people report dehydration, fatigue, skin conditions and lost productivity, and hard trade-offs between protecting health and keeping a livelihood.

What young people told us

Heat touches everything

At the youth co-design workshop, participants described heat as deeply connected to every part of life. They also said they are among the most affected by climate change, yet least represented in the discussions that decide the response.

  • Physical health
  • Mental wellbeing
  • Menstrual health
  • Livelihoods
  • Food systems
  • Water security
  • Social relationships

They are already adapting: changing work hours and diets, using protective clothing and seeking shade, while asking for better information and stronger community support.

A youth-responsive ecosystem

For, of and by young people

Young people should be recognised as knowledge holders, community responders and policy actors, not only as beneficiaries.

For

Systems that protect youth wellbeing

Stronger public health responses, climate-responsive places to learn, and protection for young people's livelihoods.

Of

Meaningful participation in governance

Youth-informed heat action planning, local governance platforms, and young people recognised as stakeholders.

By

Youth-led adaptation

Building on what young people already do, valuing traditional knowledge, and spreading awareness through peer networks.

The roadmap

Advancing youth-responsive heat resilience

Shaped with young people from across Karnataka, the roadmap moves from isolated interventions to a coordinated ecosystem where institutions, communities and young people build resilience together.

Short term · 1 to 2 years
  • Heat-health awareness among young people
  • Youth perspectives in heat planning
  • Better data on youth heat vulnerability
  • Promoting local adaptation knowledge
Medium term · 3 to 5 years
  • Institutionalising youth participation
  • Climate-responsive learning environments
  • Climate-sensitive livelihoods
  • Climate-sensitive infrastructure
Long term · beyond 5 years
  • Youth embedded in climate governance
  • Integrated climate and health planning
  • Strengthening traditional knowledge systems
  • Intergenerational climate leadership
Conclusion
Karnataka's response to rising heat cannot be designed for young people without actively engaging young people themselves.

There is no single youth experience of heat in Karnataka. The most significant finding is the gap between what young people live through and how climate governance is organised. Closing that gap is not only a matter of inclusion or equity: it is fundamental to effective climate governance.

A resilient future needs policies that protect youth, institutions that include youth, and governance that trusts and enables youth leadership.
